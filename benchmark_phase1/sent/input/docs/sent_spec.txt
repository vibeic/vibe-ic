Single Edge Nibble Transmission (SENT) — Automotive Sensor Interface
SAE J2716 (SENT — Single Edge Nibble Transmission for Automotive Applications)

ABSTRACT

Single Edge Nibble Transmission (SENT) is a unidirectional, point-to-point,
single-signal-wire digital interface defined by SAE J2716 for transmitting
high-resolution sensor data from a smart sensor to an electronic control unit
(ECU) in automotive applications. SENT is a three-wire interface: a single
signal line (SENT signal), a supply voltage line, and a ground line. Data is
conveyed on the single signal wire by the time between successive falling
edges, measured in unit time intervals called ticks. Each nibble (four bits)
is encoded as a pulse whose period, in ticks, represents the nibble value.
SENT is used for sensors such as pressure, temperature, position, mass air
flow, and throttle position. Because SENT carries the data as pulse-width
(falling-edge to falling-edge) nibble timing on one wire, it needs no clock
line, no addressing, and no bus arbitration. A related synchronous variant,
SENT-SPC (Short PWM Code / Single edge nibble transmission for Push-Pull),
adds a master trigger pulse from the ECU so the transmission becomes
synchronous and addressable.

This document specifies the SENT signal encoding, the message frame format
(synchronization/calibration pulse, status and serial communication nibble,
data nibbles, and CRC nibble), the slow serial communication channel (short
serial message and enhanced serial message), the SENT-SPC variant, and the
timing tolerances.

1. OVERVIEW

SENT (SAE J2716) defines how a transmitting device (the sensor) sends a
continuous stream of message frames to a receiving device (the ECU) over a
single signal wire. The interface is unidirectional: the sensor only
transmits, and the ECU only receives. There is no request from the ECU in the
basic (asynchronous) SENT mode; the sensor transmits frames back-to-back.

The physical interface uses three wires:
  - SENT signal line: the single data-carrying wire, push-pull driven,
    nominal levels 0 V (low) and 5 V (high).
  - Supply voltage line: 5 V supply to the sensor.
  - Ground line: common ground reference.

The signal is a sequence of pulses. Each pulse begins with a falling edge,
stays low for a fixed minimum low time, then returns high, and the next pulse
begins at the next falling edge. The information is carried in the TIME
between two successive falling edges. This falling-edge-to-falling-edge timing
is the defining feature of SENT: data is encoded as pulse width (nibble pulse
period) measured in ticks, not as voltage levels and not as bit cells clocked
by a separate clock.

2. UNIT TIME — THE TICK

The fundamental unit of time in SENT is the tick. The tick (also called the
unit time or clock tick) has a nominal duration of 3 microseconds, and the
specification permits a tick period in the range of 3 microseconds to 90
microseconds. The transmitter's actual tick time may drift; the receiver
recovers the effective tick time from the synchronization/calibration pulse
at the start of every frame. All nibble pulse periods are expressed as an
integer number of ticks.

The transmitter tick tolerance is up to +/- 20 percent of nominal in the
basic specification; the receiver measures the synchronization/calibration
pulse to compensate for this drift on a per-frame basis. Tick variation
within a single message frame is bounded so that the receiver can use the
measured calibration tick time for all nibbles of that frame.

3. NIBBLE ENCODING

Data in SENT is transmitted four bits at a time. A four-bit unit is called a
nibble. Each nibble is sent as one pulse. The nibble pulse period, measured in
ticks from one falling edge to the next falling edge, encodes the nibble
value:

  nibble pulse period (ticks) = 12 + nibble_value

where nibble_value ranges from 0 to 15. Therefore:
  - A nibble value of 0 is a pulse of 12 ticks.
  - A nibble value of 15 is a pulse of 27 ticks.

The pulse always begins with a falling edge and includes a minimum low period
(typically 5 ticks) followed by a high period; the total falling-edge to
falling-edge period determines the nibble value. The fixed 12-tick offset
guarantees a minimum pulse period and provides a non-zero floor so that a
nibble value of zero is still a measurable pulse.

4. MESSAGE FRAME FORMAT

A SENT message frame is a sequence of pulses transmitted in the following
order:

  1. Synchronization/Calibration pulse
  2. Status and Serial Communication nibble
  3. Data nibbles (1 to 6 nibbles)
  4. CRC nibble (Cyclic Redundancy Check, 4-bit)
  5. Optional pause pulse

4.1 Synchronization/Calibration Pulse

Every frame begins with a synchronization/calibration pulse with a nominal
period of 56 ticks (falling edge to falling edge). The receiver measures the
actual duration of this 56-tick pulse to determine the effective tick time for
the current frame, compensating for transmitter clock drift. Dividing the
measured calibration pulse duration by 56 yields the per-frame tick time used
to decode all subsequent nibbles in the frame.

4.2 Status and Serial Communication Nibble

The pulse immediately after the synchronization/calibration pulse is the
status and serial communication nibble. This is a single 4-bit nibble (a
pulse of 12 + value ticks). Two of its bits carry status information and two
of its bits (bit 2 and bit 3) carry one bit each of the slow serial
communication channel, accumulated across many consecutive frames to build a
serial message.

4.3 Data Nibbles

After the status nibble, the frame carries between one and six data nibbles.
Each data nibble is a pulse of 12 + value ticks and represents four bits of
sensor data. A typical SENT sensor transmits two independent 12-bit data
channels: data channel 1 in the first three nibbles and data channel 2 in the
last three nibbles, for a total of six data nibbles. Other configurations (for
example a single 12-bit channel plus a counter, or a 16-bit channel) use
fewer or differently grouped nibbles. The number of data nibbles is fixed for
a given sensor configuration and known to the ECU.

4.4 CRC Nibble

The last data-bearing pulse of a frame is the CRC nibble: a single 4-bit
nibble that carries a 4-bit Cyclic Redundancy Check (CRC-4) computed over the
data nibbles (and, in the enhanced/recommended implementation, over the status
nibble). The CRC-4 uses a 4-bit polynomial with a defined seed; the receiver
recomputes the CRC over the received nibbles and compares it against the
transmitted CRC nibble to detect transmission errors. The standard defines a
lookup-table-based CRC-4 calculation.

4.5 Optional Pause Pulse

A frame may be followed by an optional pause pulse so that the total frame
length (including pause) is a constant number of ticks. The pause pulse lets
the system produce a fixed frame period regardless of the data values, which
simplifies ECU scheduling. When no pause pulse is used, frames are transmitted
back-to-back and the frame period varies with the data.

5. SLOW SERIAL COMMUNICATION CHANNEL (SLOW CHANNEL)

In addition to the fast channel (the data nibbles transmitted every frame),
SENT defines a slow serial communication channel carried in the status and
serial communication nibble across many frames. Two forms are defined:

5.1 Short Serial Message

The Short Serial Message uses bit 2 of the status nibble as a serial data bit
and bit 3 as a frame/start indicator. Sixteen consecutive frames assemble one
16-bit short serial message containing a 4-bit message ID, an 8-bit data
field, and a 4-bit CRC. The short serial message conveys slowly changing
information such as a sensor identifier or diagnostic code.

5.2 Enhanced Serial Message

The Enhanced Serial Message assembles a longer message (over a larger number
of frames, typically 18 frames) carrying a configuration/identification
payload — for example a 12-bit or 16-bit data value with an extended message
ID and CRC. The enhanced serial message format is used for sensor
configuration, manufacturer identification, and diagnostic data.

The slow channel does not interfere with the fast data channel; it simply
reuses two bits of the status nibble each frame, so its throughput is a small
fraction of a bit per frame.

6. SENT-SPC VARIANT (SHORT PWM CODE)

SENT-SPC (Single edge nibble transmission for Push-Pull, also called Short PWM
Code) is a synchronous, addressable variant. In SPC mode the ECU initiates a
transmission by driving a master trigger pulse on the signal line; the sensor
then responds with a standard SENT frame (synchronization/calibration pulse,
status nibble, data nibbles, CRC nibble). The master trigger pulse width can
select which of several data channels the sensor returns, making SPC
addressable. SPC turns the otherwise free-running unidirectional SENT stream
into a request/response, synchronous exchange while preserving the
tick/nibble encoding and the message frame format.

7. TIMING TOLERANCES

  - Tick (unit time): nominal 3 microseconds; range 3 to 90 microseconds.
  - Synchronization/calibration pulse: nominal 56 ticks.
  - Nibble pulse period: 12 + value ticks (12 to 27 ticks), value 0 to 15.
  - Minimum nibble low time: nominal 5 ticks.
  - Transmitter tick tolerance: up to +/- 20 percent (basic); the receiver
    recalibrates from the 56-tick synchronization/calibration pulse each
    frame.
  - The receiver decodes a nibble by measuring the falling-edge to
    falling-edge period, dividing by the calibrated tick time, and
    subtracting 12.

8. RECEIVER DECODING SEQUENCE

  1. Detect the synchronization/calibration pulse (a pulse of approximately 56
     ticks, longer than any nibble pulse).
  2. Measure the calibration pulse duration and divide by 56 to obtain the
     per-frame tick time.
  3. For each following pulse, measure its falling-edge to falling-edge
     period, divide by the tick time, round to the nearest integer, and
     subtract 12 to obtain the nibble value (0 to 15).
  4. Decode the status nibble, then the data nibbles, then the CRC nibble.
  5. Recompute the CRC-4 over the data nibbles and compare with the received
     CRC nibble; flag a frame error on mismatch.
  6. Accumulate bit 2 / bit 3 of the status nibble into the slow serial
     channel message.

9. ERROR DETECTION

SENT detects errors through:
  - The CRC-4 nibble (mismatch indicates a corrupted frame).
  - Out-of-range nibble periods (a measured period outside 12 to 27 ticks, or
     a calibration pulse not near 56 ticks, indicates a framing error).
  - Successive frame consistency checks in the ECU (a value changing
     implausibly between frames).
  - Loss of the synchronization/calibration pulse (no ~56-tick pulse seen),
     causing the receiver to resynchronize at the next calibration pulse.

10. USE CASES

SENT is used wherever a smart sensor must report a high-resolution value to an
ECU over a cheap single wire without the cost of an analog wire or a full bus:
  - Pressure sensors (manifold, fuel rail, brake).
  - Position sensors (throttle, pedal, gear).
  - Mass air flow and temperature sensors.
  - Torque and angle sensors.

11. RELATIONSHIP TO OTHER AUTOMOTIVE INTERFACES

SENT differs from other single-wire automotive interfaces:
  - It is NOT LIN: LIN is a UART-based, bidirectional, multi-node serial bus
    with a master schedule, a break field, a sync field, and a protected
    identifier (PID); SENT is a unidirectional, point-to-point, pulse-width
    nibble-timed sensor link with no UART framing, no PID, and no bus.
  - It is NOT a generic PWM sensor output: PWM encodes a value as a duty cycle
    of a fixed-period square wave; SENT encodes successive 4-bit nibbles as
    falling-edge-to-falling-edge tick counts and frames them with a
    synchronization/calibration pulse and a CRC nibble.
  - It is NOT DALI: DALI is a Manchester-coded bidirectional lighting-control
    bus; SENT is a pulse-width nibble sensor interface.

The defining structural signature of SENT (SAE J2716) is: a single signal
wire carrying nibble pulses whose falling-edge-to-falling-edge period is
12 + value ticks, framed by a 56-tick synchronization/calibration pulse, a
status/serial-communication nibble, one to six data nibbles, and a 4-bit CRC
nibble, with a slow serial channel and an optional SENT-SPC synchronous
trigger variant.
